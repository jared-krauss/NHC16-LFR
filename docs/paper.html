The London Met's First Live Facial Recognition Deployment: The Trial at Notting Hill Carnival, 2016

"Facial Recognition is real life. And real life is difficult, says NEC", this was the headline for an October 2017 article by Joseph Brookes, in the online, Australian industry and technology magazine, Which-50. Speaking at their own corporate event, NEC's Head of Facial Recognition Solutions Chris de Silva is quoted saying, "Life is not an e-gate. We don't live in an e-gate" (Brookes). He's described as bragging about the superiority of their proprietary facial recognition software, NeoFace, as he points to the USA's National Institute for Standards & Technology's March 2017 publication, "Face In Video Evaluation (FIVE) Face Recognition of Non-Cooperative Subjects" (NISTIR 8173), where NEC achieved top marks in several live video surveillance experiments (Hilvert; Grother et al. 32).

From a corporate perspective, this was great marketing, and helpful in landing contracts. A 2018 FOI seeking information on the costs of Met's trial deployments of facial recogniton surveillance, filed by Big Brother Watch, showed that by the end of Fiscal Year 2016/2017, the Met Police had spent approximately £82.8K on facial recognition software and hardware (Metropolitan Police Service). And by the end of August 2018, the Met Police had spent approximately £200k total on software and hardware for facial recognition surveillance (Metropolitan Police Service).

The official first year for the Notting Hill Carnival is disputed, ranging from 1964 to 1966 ("In Pictures"; "History"). So in August 2016, during the Bank Holiday, Notting Hill Carnival began to unfold for it's third and final 50th Anniversary. On that Sunday, the early rising revellers set off for Notting Hill despite morning rain ("Rain Fails") for J'Ouvert, where they come away covered in paint from head to toe. Meanwhile, officers from the London Met were improvising their first test of NEC's NeoFace facial recognition surveillance software. Because their plan to use an unnamed transport hub was scuttled "by circumstances outside of [their] control" (National Physical Laboratory and Metropolitan Police Service 17) they were forced to place their cameras on large booms which officers held out above the crowd.

In Fig. 3, the MPS has provided a diagram of their deployment set up that day. They don't name their specific location. They placed two cameras on a single side of the road, facing the same general direction, without over lapping their fields-of-view and "Zones of Recognition" (Fig. 2). As people walk into and out of frame, they are more or less in focus, and it is only in a certain limited focal plane that they can be scanned, and a face print generated to be checked against the watchlist database.

On the day, they were using two, now discontinued, outdoor surveillance cameras from Axis (see Fig. 5), with a 1/2" sensor size (Axis Communications), and a zoom lens, roughly equivalent to 24mm to 55mm ona full-frame sensor. Those cameras were hardwired into a tent, where the watchlist database was stored locally, and all images were processed locally. Matches were kept for up to 31 days, and then deleted (National Physical Laboratory and Metropolitan Police Service 10). The video footage from each deployment was used to estimate the number of faces scanned, as well as to count the number of times someone on "the Bluelist" (a known police officer) could have been recognized, versus the number of matches (National Physical Laboratory and Metropolitan Police Service 12). The video footage was further used to make screenshots demonstrating the view from each deployment, with faces blurred (Fig. 3).

The watchlist size was 266, while the number of faces scanned over a twelve-hour cumultative period, covering both days of the festival, was counted approximately by the Met by reviewing the video footage after each deployment. In 2016, the MPS estimated there were 15,900 ‘recognition opportunities,’ with only 1 alert from their watchlist. Meanwhile, 'recognition opportunities' from the Bluelist were 56 with only 3o correct identifications (Fig. 3).

Now, this was the first official deployment by the MPS of NEC's NeoFace software, and certainly underwhelming from an operational perspective, the MPS saw it as a proof of concept (National Physical Laboratory and Metropolitan Police Service 17).

By 2017, the second deployment of NeoFace, the MPS had already iterated on their deployment tactics, from a technical perspective, but were met with much greater public backlash, meaning further deployments to Notting Hill did not occur again until 2025 (citations needed for NPL, and Met Deployment 2025).

During these trial periods, and in the intervening years, the MPS has prioritised mobile deployments of their facial recognition systems, but beginning in 2025 and continuing in 2026, more trials were undertaken to test the installation of permanent, officer-initiated facial recognition surveillance. It stands to reason then, that more permanent installations of cameras capable of facial recognition will be installed.

As the Which-50 article said, "Real life is hard." The MPS quickly realized the need to prioritize which types of spaces they inhabited, considering the flow of people through the space, as well as the need to increase the quality and number of cameras, and preplan their placements (NPL). So too have I found real life doesn't let you get on the way you hoped most of the time.

Yet, contrary to what I believed before I started my research, this was in fact not the first time that facial recognition was deployed at scale. I recently found an exchange from November 1998, when the then MP for Lewes, Norman Baker, asked Tony Blair's new Home Secretary, Jack Straw, to explain "what assessment he has made of the civil liberties implications of (a) the use of and (b) the safeguards applicable to the operation of the computer-controlled surveillance system in Newham." (Straw)

I now begin, in earnest, my research into the deeper history of facial recognition surveillance in London, beginning in the 1990s, in Newham, to see what we can learn about the present day use of live facial recognition surveillance from the first attempt at it in London, undertaken by the Borough of Newham. How will the surveillance of space through automated facial recognition systems change the way people flow through it, and how will spaces be changed to facilitate the flow of people in a way advantageous to their surveillance? We shall see.
