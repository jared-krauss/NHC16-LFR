A London History of Facial Recognition Surveillance: Walworth Road & East Street Market, November 2024

Jared Krauss 26 September 2026

Shortly after 4PM, on 1 November 2024, as I walked down Walworth Road towards Morrisons to buy groceries, I spotted a van which looked out of the ordinary, and I immediately recognized it. I rushed home, forgetting Morrisons, and grabbed my camera and returned to the junction of Walworth Road and East Street Market, where a Metropolitan Police Live Facial Recognition (LFR) van was parked just off the road on the pavement in front of what was then a Costa Coffee and is now a Greggs. The van had six cameras on its roof: two at the front, three on an elevated rail in the middle, and one at the back. On each side of the van was a peel away sticker designating it as an LFR van, stating, “LIVE FACIAL RECOGNITION IN OPERATION.”

The deployment was staffed by both uniformed and plain-clothes officers, drawn from two teams: a specialist LFR team and local officers. The Met’s policy names the specialist roles, including an LFR Operator, LFR System Engineers with “enhanced technical training,” and LFR Engagement Officers, who “may be deployed in uniform or plain clothes,” all under a Bronze Commander on site (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS Overt LFR Policy 12–13).

While I slowly orbited the scene with my camera, I was eventually approached by one ranking officer from the local team, who asked if I had any questions, and I periodically returned to him when I had some.[1] He explained, as the Met’s published documents also indicate,[2] that the scans are only processed locally, in the van, and never sent to a server. Any faces which are not matches have their biometric data deleted immediately and automatically after being scanned, and only the biometric data for matches is stored and later deleted. The Met’s own policy adds that all of the deployment’s CCTV footage is kept for up to 31 days from the date of that particular deployment (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS Overt LFR Policy 6).

No officers from the LFR team spoke to me, except for when the ranking officer asked one of them for a minute of their time. The LFR officer who predominantly resided inside the van during my time documenting the deployment, upon hearing the ranking officer ask for a moment, stepped out and closed the side door behind themselves. Upon hearing my question, "any chance I could ever do a ride along?" they laughed and suggested I email that question to their supervisor.

This was not the only time they deployed at Walworth Road. The Metropolitan Police publish a record for all of their LFR deployments on their website. There have been nine deployments at Walworth Road in total, with one before 1 November 2024 and seven after.

The first deployment, on 21 June 2024, was at the exact same junction. It lasted five hours and twelve minutes, with an estimated 1,684 faces seen and a minimum threshold setting of 0.6, and produced 9 alerts: 8 confirmed and 1 unconfirmed, which I take to mean the person could not be stopped. Five of those alerts led to arrests (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024).

Their entry for 1 November reports that the deployment lasted six hours and twenty-one minutes, with a minimum match threshold of 0.64 against a watchlist of 13,419 people, and estimates 10,510 faces were seen that night (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024).

The justification recorded for the deployment was ‘Crime Hotspot’, one of the use cases defined in the Met’s LFR policy, as an area "approximately 300-500m across where crime data ... MPS intelligence reporting and/or operational experience as to future criminality indicates that that it is an area where: (i) the crime rate; and/or (ii) the rate at which crime in that area is rising, is assessed to be in the upper quartile for that BCU/OCU area” (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS Overt LFR Policy 4).

Though it was 1 November, this was not the first deployment of LFR for November 2024. According to the same Met deployment document, two deployments took place on 1 November 2024, the first in Romford and the second on Walworth Road. They were, respectively, the 151st and 152nd of the 180 deployments the Met listed for 2024 (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024).[3] And from the very first deployment in 2016, this 1 November, 2024 Walworth Road deployment was the 194th LFR deployment in London by the Metropolitan Police.

While on a deployment the LFR vans are stabilised with what appears to be hydraulic legs. I presume this has operational advantages. First, officers were seen entering and exiting multiple times, which would normally cause vibrations, and secondly, being deployed at night they are likely in need of every photographic technical advantage they can utilize, so a stabilised platform means no camera shake at the lowest usable shutter speed, which in my experience as a photographer is likely going to be around 1/200th or 1/250th of a second. In other words, the more stable the platform, the better image they can capture and cleaner biometric data to assess, the higher probability they have of a successful match, allowing them to—as the NEC slogan goes—react.

This theory would be supported by the technical specs last reported for the cameras used. The Met has not published the camera hardware used in 2024. The most recent specific account I have found is in its report on the 2016–2019 trials, where the 2016 and 2017 Notting Hill Carnival deployments used Axis Q1635 cameras (2 megapixels, fitted with 9–80 mm zoom lenses) (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 29), a model Axis rates for shutter speeds from 1/34,500 of a second to two seconds and which has a ½" sized sensor, which is good for security cameras, but many modern phones have larger sensors for their main cameras in 2026 (Axis Communications).

While I continued to circle the van, with the intention of a documentary-photography-inspired approach to 3D documentation with my camera, which I subsequently made using Gaussian Splatting and published online (Krauss, London Facial Recognition Surveillance), I observed most of the local officers congregated and conversed amongst each other, though some stood largely still, watching the phone in their hand and waiting for an alert with bated breath (Krauss, Alert on Phone).

While documenting the deployment, one male citizen was identified as a match (Krauss, Alert on Phone) with their watchlist, subsequently stopped and searched (Krauss, Looking Backwards). Though he was led away, I cannot be sure he was arrested. I did not see him be placed into a van or walked to the nearby station. There were other alerts that evening, and the Met’s reported figures of three arrests and eight cases of No Further Action (NFA) cannot tell me which group he fell into.

According to the Met’s data, there were no misidentifications during that deployment (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024). However, the Met recorded 10 alerts that night, all logged as true alerts with no false alerts; yet its outcome columns record 3 arrests and 8 cases of NFA, a total of 11 (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024). The Met does not explain this discrepancy. Nor does it follow the format of the Met’s 2023 and early-2024 records. In the Met’s 2023 and early-2024 format, the last two outcome columns (‘other disposal’ and ‘no action’) always carry the same figure, and should not be counted together, lest their data not align. So, for Walworth Road's first LFR deployment, 21 June, 2024, there were nine alerts, resulting in five arrests made, and the 'other disposal' and 'no action' columns both report a figure of four, which suggests it should be counted once, making 5 + 4 = 9. Yet in the later-2024 format that doubling disappears (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024). Therefore, for 1 November, 2024, it is unclear whether there were in fact only ten true alerts, or whether one person triggered two alerts and was twice released without further action during this 1 November 2024 Walworth Road deployment, or whether it is a transcription error (Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024).

By my count of the Met's own records, deployments numbered 1 in 2016, 2 in 2017, 5 in 2018 and 2 in 2019; 3 in 2020, none in 2021 and 6 in 2022; then 23 in 2023, 180 in 2024 and 231 in 2025 (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 7; Metropolitan Police Service, LFR Deployment Grid 2020–2022; Metropolitan Police Service, MPS LFR Deployments 2023–2024; Metropolitan Police Service, Live Facial Recognition Deployment Record 2025). By the year ending 2025, I count 453 deployment entries in the Met Police's records. They've scanned at least five-million faces in that time, and made approximately 1,761 arrests, and report at least 146 false alerts.

The software the Met has used throughout this time is NeoFace, made by the Japanese multinational NEC Corporation. The Met’s 2020 trials report never names NEC, only “NeoFace” and its version numbers, but NeoFace is NEC’s product, as NEC’s own brochure makes plain (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 29; NEC Corporation).[4] But the March 2023 NPL study explicitly names NEC as the creator of the fourth iteration of NeoFace (Mansfield). The NEC software's full name is NeoFace Watch, which, understandably, is sometimes confused with a UK produced LFR product increasingly being adopted by UK retailers, called FaceWatch. In December 2015, the BBC’s Chris Vallance reported on Facewatch, Simon Gordon’s “thief recognition” system then only on trial in UK shops, in an article titled “Facewatch ‘Thief Recognition’ CCTV on Trial in UK Stores” (Vallance). Simon Gordon is the owner of Gordon's Wine Bar near Embankment Station, whose website states that you agree to the use of facial recognition when on their premises.

The same BBC piece looked back to June 2015, when Leicestershire Police used NEC’s NeoFace software to scan festival-goers at Download Festival in Donington Park, drawing an on-stage protest from the headlining band Muse. Towards the end of “Uprising,” the band’s singer, Matt Bellamy, took to the microphone to shout, “Fuck the Leicestershire police for scanning your faces without you [garbled]!”[5] The protest can still be watched on YouTube (“MUSE Download 2015”).

This was one of the earliest public uses of LFR in the UK. And at the festival, NEC's software was used. It was then also used at the 2016 and the 2017 Notting Hill Carnival trials, and at the 2017 Remembrance Sunday event in Whitehall. These early trials set the stage, and established the working methods the Met Police still use today.

The 2016 Notting Hill Carnival deployment of LFR by the Metropolitan Police, being the first ever public trial of LFR in London, was, by its own numbers, ineffective, though the Met’s words put a positive spin on it. They write, “Although there were no positive identifications against the watchlist, the algorithm performance under such challenging conditions, combined with the use of the technology by officers, demonstrated the potential of LFR” (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 17). Their numbers tell a different story: over twelve hours, across two days, with a watchlist of 266 people and approximately 15,900 “recognition opportunities,” the system produced a single alert, and no interactions between citizens and police are recorded (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 17).

Compare this with their second live trial in London, at the 2017 Notting Hill Carnival, where they applied lessons learned in 2016. Over the two days there were approximately 101,000 “recognition opportunities,” the report’s term for faces scanned, producing 96 alerts, of which only one was confirmed correct, with no arrests (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 17). That one correct alert was for a person who, in the report’s words, had been “dealt with the previous week” but was still on the watchlist. The report also puts a positive spin on the watchlist mistake: “This trial identified the need for the process to be overhauled to ensure that watchlists can be produced sufficiently quickly to ensure their reasonable currency” (Metropolitan Police Service and National Physical Laboratory 18).

Clearly the number of deployments is rising sharply. And I have asked myself basic questions about the adoption and rapidly expanding use of this cutting edge technology, such as: is this the best the Met Police can do to be transparent about the LFR data, or is their data intentionally not consistently formatted in its reporting to make more difficult statistical use of their reporting? Or, another concern I've carried around in my head is, is this the most urgent use by the State of cutting edge technology? A subjective question, but it opens up another, perhaps more valuable critique which I believe is left out of the conversation far too often. When it comes to adopting emerging technologies, especially those powered by machine learning, computer vision, artificial intelligence, etc., is LFR the best use of government resources for improving the lived experience of individuals on the streets they are surveilling? Despite this question still being an essentially subjective one, I find more value in it because it destabilizes the implied assumption in the questioning of the police using LFR, which is often implied as, “Of course this is a very powerful tool law enforcement should be using.”

The creators at NEC of the “Capture, Assess, Match, React” slogan, as printed in a NeoFace Watch brochure downloaded from NEC’s website in 2019 (NEC Corporation), Simon Gordon at FaceWatch, and those developing policing methodologies around various uses of Facial Recognition Surveillance are not questioning the fundamental purpose of law enforcement as it exists, in this instance, in the UK. They already assume the police should be using LFR and should expand their LFR operations. Whereas other critics fundamentally question the role and usefulness of the police in British society, such as Alex Vitale in The End of Policing (2017) and A World Without Police by Geo Maher (2021). My pondering upon the potential best uses of State resources to improve the daily lived experience of citizens also does not assume that the police should have LFR capabilities, nor that the government should prioritize developing practical applications of emerging technologies for the advancement of law enforcement activities when there are many socio-economic qualities to our society which could receive funding and attention from the State to reduce crime.

But leaving aside historical criticisms of police as a form of social control rather than creators of public safety, as well as questions of restorative justice and abolitionist pursuits, dealing just with the situation as it is, there are still, very simple privacy concerns I have, without even needing to be super technical. For instance, the phone application the Met use is of interest to me with regards to privacy concerns. I have not been allowed permission to photograph, and so only have a blurry photograph of it in action (Krauss, Alert on Phone). I have not even been told the name of the application when I asked in person. The officer told me he'd get in trouble if I was even shown the app, perhaps due to GDPR concerns, as an alert could come up at any moment. So I cannot confirm if the Met uses NEC's application for their NeoFace suite, or their own internal proprietary application.

Yet the following are questions I have not found an answer to in the publicly available documentation on the Metropolitan Police’s website, such as their Standard Operating Procedure for LFR, which only mentions phone boxes and does not address officer use of phones and the application: Is this app something that only specific phones provided by the Met are able to access and utilize? Are there temporary logins provided during a deployment or permanent logins to the app, so every officer’s actions are documented? Is this application always available on the individual work phones of officers? Is it available on their own personal devices? Regardless of what device the application is on, I’m curious how they prevent, for instance, screenshots being taken and shared; in my research I have not found an answer to that question.

I have now directly observed and documented six different sites where the Met Police used LFR, and have come to recognize some of the individuals I presume are on the LFR team. One question that has begun to nag at me arose during August, 2026. Over the two days of the 2026 edition of the Notting Hill Carnival, where I documented four different sites, I am certain some of those individuals also came to recognize me. One person I recognized from the day before and from 2024, based on their reaction on the second day—doing a double take upon sighting me—also recognized me. This individual was seemingly always on a video call, and several times during their video call I was, perhaps inadvertently, in the background, over their shoulder while I went about documenting the LFR deployment.[6]

The basic question nagging at me is, is Retrospective Facial Recognition (RFR) used to support Live Facial Recognition (LFR)? For instance, in the course of their duties, if they are on a video call with a supervisor or a peer, an officer is allowed to record that video call, and would not need to seek out my permission to do so simply because I am in the background, as we’re both standing in public. But are they able to then run that video through their RFR systems to identify me outside of their general LFR watchlist, which I would not be on?[7] Their own RFR document doesn't rule this out, and, as ever, the question is a subjective one. On page 6 of the Data Protection Impact Assessment for RFR they list several benefits of RFR, among them “analysing media obtained from open source platforms” and “Help to identify persons who are unwilling to identify themselves ... where powers to take fingerprints are not available. As well as assisting in an investigative context, this is also relevant to policing in a protective security contact [sic] too” (Metropolitan Police Service, Data Protection Impact Assessment 6).

It is not outside the realm of possibility for an officer to want to identify me out of a concern about my activities documenting these LFR deployments. When we consider the “See it. Say it. Sorted.” posters prevalent in London, depicting an individual photographing the surveillance system as something worth being reported to the police. Add to this the fact that I was stopped while photographing in the vicinity of Wimbledon station, during the tournament in 2019 and told that my making photos near the train station was justification enough to stop and search me, as well as my aforementioned distrust and/or skepticism of the police generally, I think it is understandable why my brain might wonder if a specialist member of the LFR team would want to try and identify me, and use a concern about the security context of LFR deployment, or the Carnival, as the pretense.

The London Metropolitan Police began their use of LFR in 2016, with just the single deployment. By the end of 2025, more than 4 million faces had been scanned in just that year alone. The number of deployments keeps increasing, as does the number of faces scanned. Yet public discussion is minimal, and public awareness, based on my own interactions for the last couple years in London, while researching this, few are really aware it's being used, let alone that it is being used so prevalently.

I saw one man stopped on Walworth Road, and I'll never know what emotions were crossing his face throughout the moment, but I thought I saw a mixture of anger and sadness, before a look of forlornness came over him. We will see more, and it will begin to become common place, if the Met continue their expansion of LFR. In 2026 the BTP began trialling LFR at train stations and London Underground stations (British Transport Police). Within the Greater London city limits, there is approximately 9 million residents. In one year, the Met Police managed to scan more than 4 million faces, and, with the BTP joining in, the numbers are on track to scan more in 2026.

In that BBC article from 2015, towards the end, the then Surveillance Camera Commissioner, Tony Porter, posed a hypothetical, which I don't believe the public has truly wrestled with, and which I pose once more here in closing, "The public need to ask themselves: do they want six million cameras painted red at head height looking at them?" (Vallance). The police have gone out of their way to avoid such aesthetics, and their rollout of permanent LFR hardware has been slow, beginning with two cameras on North End, in central Croydon, in 2025 (Klovig Skelton), but it continues apace. Before too long, every station could be using LFR to scan each passenger.

And now, I return to some of my basic questions to close, was policing the first and best use of facial recognition technology (FRT) to improve the daily lives of citizens in London? How else could FRT or other emerging technologies been adapted to support the everyday, largely inconsequential citizen? Could we have received a discount on TfL fares if we used a FRT gate instead of tap and go? Could we use FRT at elections instead of requiring physical ID? How did we arrive at millions of faces scanned each year, and an unknown, but likely substantial, financial investment into LFR without similar, public examples of emerging technology being deployed by the State in other facets of our daily life?

Perhaps the most sane question, though, in all my research, one which would make Benjamin Franklin proud comes from a local Croydon resident named Magnus, as quoted in an East London Lines article, speaking about the installation of permanent LFR cameras in Croydon. He is first quoted saying, "we shouldn’t have to be relying upon cameras to make areas safer," then saying "...a higher concentration of socioeconomic issues, which this country fails to look at” leads to persistent issues for which "short-sighted solutions" are sought, such as FRT. The article hints that he draws comparison between mass surveillance by the state and mass harvesting of personal data by large tech corporations, before closing with the direct question that would have Franklin nodding along: “Just because the cameras can pick up some criminals, why should everyone else’s right to privacy be harmed?” (Kunda).
